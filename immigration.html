Immigration

A controlled, fair immigration system that works for the people of Britain — with managed borders, honest debate, and rules that are clear, consistent and actually enforced.

An honest conversation Britain has been avoiding

Immigration is one of the most important policy questions facing Britain today — and one of the most dishonestly debated. For years, political parties across the spectrum have either dismissed public concern as bigotry, or turned it into a culture war rather than a serious policy conversation. Neither approach has served the British public.

Sherrie De Kiguchi is married into a Japanese family. She is not hostile to immigration. She understands, personally, what it means to build a life that bridges cultures. But she also lives in a community in Hampshire where she can see and hear what unmanaged, uncontrolled, and poorly integrated immigration does to services, to housing, to public safety, and to social cohesion.

Her position is simple: Britain should be a country that welcomes those who can contribute, that treats people with dignity, and that has the confidence to manage its own borders. That is not a radical position. It is a sensible one.

"You can support managed immigration and oppose open borders. You can welcome people who come here to contribute and also demand that our system is fair and controlled. These are not contradictions. They are common sense."

The link to child protection

Sherrie does not treat immigration as an isolated issue. The grooming gang scandals — in Rotherham, Rochdale, and towns across Britain — revealed how badly the political class had failed to address the intersection of organised criminality, community breakdown, and institutional cowardice. The fear of being labelled racist was used as a reason to ignore the rape of children.

That experience is part of why Sherrie insists on honest debate about immigration. When legitimate concerns are dismissed or labelled, the conversation goes underground — and the people who suffer most are the most vulnerable. An open, fact-based conversation about immigration policy is not just about borders. It is about the kind of society we are willing to build and defend.

What Sherrie is calling for

  • An annual cap on net migration set by Parliament and voted on publicly — no more numbers decided behind closed doors
  • An end to illegal Channel crossings through offshore processing and immediate return agreements — if you arrive illegally, you do not settle here
  • A points-based system that prioritises skills Britain actually needs, with English language and self-sufficiency requirements before settlement
  • Automatic deportation of any foreign national convicted of a serious crime, with the Human Rights Act disapplied in deportation cases
  • Citizenship treated as a privilege — earned through contribution, integration, and respect for British law and values
